The Amazon doesn't have summer and winter so much as *up* and *down*. Around Manaus, the Rio Negro rises and falls by around ten metres a year, and that changes everything about how you travel.

## High water: canoe through the treetops

Roughly from March to July, peaking around June, the rivers spill into the forest. The *igapó*, the flooded black-water forest, becomes a maze of trunks and reflections, and you paddle through it at the height of the lower canopy.

That puts you at eye level with things you'd normally crane your neck for: monkeys moving through the branches, sloths in no hurry at all, toucans and kingfishers, orchids and bromeliads. Pink river dolphins follow the water into the trees.

**Best for:** silent canoe trips, canopy wildlife, dramatic reflections, cooler and quieter days on the water.

## Low water: walk beneath them

Roughly from September to December, with the lowest levels around October and November, the water retreats. The forest floor comes back, and with it the jungle trails, the giant roots and the tracks of animals that came to drink in the night.

White-sand beaches appear along the Rio Negro, perfect for an afternoon swim. Fishing is at its best, and birds gather along the exposed banks.

**Best for:** walking, river beaches, fishing, and seeing the structure of the forest from the ground.

## And in between?

The months between (rising water in the early part of the year, falling water around August) have their own charm: a bit of both, and often fewer visitors.

## So which should you choose?

Honestly? Both are wonderful, and they're so different that many people come back for the other one. If you dream of gliding through a flooded forest at dawn, come at high water. If you want to walk among giant trees and swim from a beach in the middle of the jungle, come at low water.
